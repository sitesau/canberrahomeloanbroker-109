Key takeaway

Most broker relationships in Canberra work well because lenders pay commission at settlement, so standard residential help usually costs the borrower nothing. The warning signs worth testing are a vague lender shortlist, no Credit Guide before credit assistance, unclear licence status, scheme figures quoted without verification, and guaranteed approval dates. Pre-approval is commonly quoted at 24 to 48 hours, and full approval at two to three weeks, so any promise that skips those steps deserves scrutiny before you sign anything.

A first meeting should reduce uncertainty, not add to it. A broker who cannot say which lenders are being compared, or why specialist lenders are or are not relevant, is leaving the central question unanswered. Equally, ask whether the broker operates under an Australian Credit Licence or as an authorised credit representative, and expect a Credit Guide before any credit assistance is given. Vague answers on these points are early mortgage brokers canberra red that are cheap to test and expensive to ignore.

Other signs include quoting scheme savings as guaranteed rather than as figures to verify, and pushing toward a lodgement before documents are complete. Fee and disclosure checks that protect you

For standard residential lending, broker services are generally free to the borrower because the lender pays commission when a loan settles. That makes fee transparency a useful screen. Ask how the commission is disclosed, whether any borrower-paid fee applies, and insist on seeing the Credit Guide before accepting assistance. ASIC Moneysmart publishes consumer information on home loan costs, repayments and interest rates that can be used to sanity-check what you are told.

  • Ask for the Credit Guide and any fee disclosure in writing.
  • Ask whether commission varies between the shortlisted lenders.
  • Compare the advice against your own income, expenses and property contract.

Scheme promises that need verification

ACT and national buyer schemes are genuine, but eligibility and timing decide whether they change your position. Quoted figures for the ACT Home Buyer Concession Scheme include no stamp duty on properties up to $1,020,000 for eligible buyers from July 2025, concessional rates up to $1,455,000, and a maximum saving of $35,238. The conditions matter: the buyer must not have owned property in Australia in the past five years and must live in the property for at least one year.

Nationally, the First Home Guarantee from October 2025 may allow a five per cent deposit without lenders mortgage insurance, and Help to Buy may allow a two per cent deposit. Treat every one of these numbers as a prompt for verification during advice, because eligibility, lender acceptance and settlement timing must line up before they alter the practical approval path. A broker who presents them as automatic is skipping that work; further background is set out in this beginner guide to broker help in Canberra.

Timing claims worth pressure-testing

Quoted timelines give you a reference point. Pre-approval is commonly expected within 24 to 48 hours, while full approval is described as taking two to three weeks depending on documentation and lender workload. A red flag is any guarantee of a date before documents are reviewed, or pressure to rely on pre-approval without knowing what must be complete first. Ask what has to be finished before pre-approval, full approval and settlement can be treated as reliable.

Who this warning list applies to

The checks above matter most where the loan purpose is clear but fit across lenders still needs testing. First home buyers are checking scheme eligibility, deposit position and whether lenders mortgage insurance applies. Refinancers are testing whether a new loan improves their position after exit costs and any changes to repayments, offsets or debt consolidation. Investors need sharper questions on interest-only options, offset accounts and structure, while construction borrowers need progressive drawdowns aligned with builder payment stages. This is a general information checklist, not personal credit advice; ASIC is Australia's consumer credit regulator and Moneysmart is the natural starting point for independent consumer information.

  1. Define the loan purpose. State whether the application is a first purchase, refinance, investment property or construction loan, since each brings different documents and decision points.
  2. List the scheme questions. Ask which ACT or national buyer schemes might apply and what proof of eligibility is required before relying on any concession.
  3. Test the lender shortlist. Have the broker explain which lenders were considered and why the shortlist matches your scenario, including whether specialist lenders are relevant.
  4. Confirm documents and timing. Check what is needed for pre-approval, full approval and settlement, and review the Credit Guide and any fee disclosure before accepting credit assistance.
